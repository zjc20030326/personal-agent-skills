# Stage contracts

Use only the stages selected in `SKILL.md`. Preserve the contracts below while adapting subject matter and depth.

## Stage 1: Five-lens map

Analyze the topic through five lenses:

1. Practitioner: operational reality, tacit knowledge, recurring failure modes.
2. Scholar: strongest peer-reviewed or otherwise authoritative evidence.
3. Skeptic: strongest serious counterargument and neglected evidence.
4. Incentives analyst: money, status, institutions, or other incentives shaping the narrative.
5. Historian: relevant precedents, recurring patterns, and where analogy breaks.

For each lens provide:

- a two-sentence thesis;
- the strongest supporting evidence and its source status;
- one insight other lenses are likely to miss;
- one observation that would weaken the thesis.

If a lens is materially irrelevant, replace it with a domain-appropriate lens such as engineer, ethicist, regulator, evaluator, or novice learner. State the substitution.

## Stage 2: Contradiction map

Identify:

- direct conflicts and the evidence behind each side;
- strongest and weakest evidence, with reasons;
- the single unresolved question with the greatest conflict-reducing value;
- claims all lenses accept;
- important omissions and possible blind spots.

Do not force disagreement when the evidence is genuinely convergent.

## Stage 3: Synthesis brief

Produce:

- a detailed 60-second summary;
- five findings ordered by reliability, naming supporting and opposing lenses;
- one cross-lens relationship invisible from a single perspective;
- one role-specific action;
- one frontier question that could materially change the synthesis.

## Stage 4: Peer-review check

For each finding:

- score reliability from 1 to 10;
- explain the score using source quality, agreement, directness, and recency;
- name the least certain claim and the evidence needed to test it;
- identify an overweighted lens;
- consider whether a sixth lens would change the conclusion;
- give the most important revision a demanding domain expert would request.

Revise material defects before moving on.

## Stage 5: Resource curation

Select at most five resources across books, papers, courses, videos, software, or communities.

For each resource state:

- why it beats the nearest alternative for this learner;
- how to use it: read, watch, practice, reproduce, or discuss;
- realistic time cost;
- the one takeaway or capability to extract;
- access, date, level, and verification status.

Use “live-verified in this turn” only after opening the resource or authoritative metadata with a tool in the current turn. Otherwise say “not live-verified.” Name overrated options or common traps only when evidence supports the warning. Arrange the chosen resources into a path that fits the stated budget; do not pad the list.

## Stage 6: Five-level learning ladder

Build five levels:

1. Complete beginner.
2. Basic understanding.
3. Practical user.
4. Problem solver.
5. Confident practitioner.

For every level specify:

- what to understand;
- observable mastery;
- priority concepts or skills;
- one promotion milestone;
- one hands-on exercise or small project;
- the common failure mode;
- one self-test question.

Make prerequisites explicit and allow diagnostic skipping when the learner can already pass a milestone.

For an interactive multi-day request, give a compact ladder and route overview, then teach only the first block. Expand later levels after the learner reaches the checkpoint.

## Stage 7: Two-hour core sprint

Identify the smallest set of concepts and skills likely to produce most practical value, and explain the selection.

Create a 120-minute route of up to 10 short lessons. For each lesson specify:

- minutes;
- objective;
- essential idea;
- one active exercise;
- one verified resource only when it adds value;
- expected result.

Teach the sprint in blocks. After each block, ask a compact retrieval check and wait when interaction is available. End with one small project that proves the target capability. If the requested outcome cannot realistically fit 2 hours, narrow the target explicitly.

## Stage 8: Adaptive oral exam

Run 10 questions with this progression:

- Questions 1–3: beginner;
- Questions 4–6: intermediate;
- Questions 7–8: advanced;
- Questions 9–10: expert.

Ask exactly one question per turn. After every response:

1. score it from 0 to 10;
2. say what was correct;
3. locate the exact gap, error, or weak link;
4. reteach the missing part plainly;
5. ask one follow-up first when the answer is weak, or raise difficulty slightly when it is strong.

At the end provide the final score, strongest and weakest areas, a short repair plan, and five final challenge questions. Do not reveal future answers.

## Stage 9: Feynman loop

1. Explain the concept using plain language and one concrete analogy.
2. Ask the learner to restate it in their own words.
3. Mark every ambiguity, skipped step, circular definition, and hidden assumption.
4. Reteach only those gaps.
5. Request a new explanation and repeat.

Do not introduce the next concept until the current explanation is simple, accurate, and complete. Note where the analogy stops matching reality.

## Stage 10: One-page retrieval sheet

Include:

- one plain-language definition;
- essential concepts, rules, formulas, or steps in short bullets;
- three to five real use cases;
- common errors and confusions;
- a pre-use checklist;
- five rapid questions without immediately exposing the answers.

Optimize for a five-minute review. Prefer retrieval cues over compressed textbook prose.
